A conference is not a hackathon. You are not there to build something in 36 hours. You are there to meet people, and the difference in how you prepare follows from that.

There are three reasons to go, and it is worth knowing which one you are chasing before you spend a weekend on it:

- **Hiring.** Some conferences run career fairs where companies interview and give offers on the spot. For an undergraduate this is by far the highest-value category.
- **Learning.** Talks, workshops, and hands-on villages, most of which you can watch online for free afterward.
- **Research networking.** Meeting the people whose papers you read. This matters if you are heading toward a PhD and much less otherwise.

## start with the recruiting ones

If you only go to one conference before you graduate, make it one from the first group below. These are the events where a student with no publications and a half-finished GitHub can walk in and leave with an interview. Everything else on this page is optional by comparison.

## research conferences

NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, CVPR, and USENIX Security come up a lot in conversation, and they are genuinely the top venues in their fields. They are also not something you attend by buying a ticket. The path runs through a lab: you join one, you work on something, you publish, and your advisor funds the travel. Registration alone is usually several hundred dollars and the value is almost entirely in presenting work rather than watching talks.

If that is the direction you want, the useful next step is not a conference list. It is the [Research guide](/guides/research), which covers finding labs, emailing faculty, and programs like FURI.

## getting funded

Almost nobody pays full price for these. Two channels are worth knowing:

- **The conference's own student program.** Most of the large ones run scholarships that cover registration and sometimes travel and lodging. These are the deadlines that actually matter, and they usually close several months before the event, well before general registration.
- **ASU funding.** Depending on your standing there are travel grants through Fulton Student Council, USG, and GPSA for graduate students. If your trip is tied to a lab, ask your advisor or department first, since research travel is often covered outright.

Apply to the scholarship early, and apply even if you think you are underqualified. These programs are trying to find students who have not been before.

## preparing

- Have a resume ready before the scholarship portal opens, not after you are accepted. See [Resume and Career Prep](/guides/resume-and-career-prep).
- Be able to pull up a project on your phone. At a career fair you get about ninety seconds and a laptop is rarely open.
- Have a short answer to "what are you working on" that does not require a whiteboard. [Interview Prep](/guides/interview-prep) covers this in more detail.
- Coordinate with other ASU students going to the same event. Ask in the [SoDA Discord](https://discord.gg/g6Nu8Fj4G6). Sharing a room makes the trip dramatically cheaper, and walking into a career fair with people you know is easier than doing it alone.

## while you are there

- Go to the smaller company booths first. The queue at the largest ones can eat an entire morning for a thirty second conversation and a sticker.
- Do not spend the whole time at the career fair. The talks are where you find out what people are actually building, and they are usually emptier during peak fair hours.
- Follow up on LinkedIn within a day or two, while the person still remembers the conversation. Mention something specific you talked about.
