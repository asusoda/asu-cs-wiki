Personal projects are a great way to learn a new stack or explore your interests, but they are usually only one part of building strong experience. Working on projects with a team, real stakeholders, or institutional support helps you develop the kinds of skills employers and graduate programs value most: defining scope, collaborating in a shared codebase, delivering to real users, and navigating ambiguity.

If you are specifically interested in research-based projects, such as joining a lab, applying for FURI, or working toward publications, see the [Research guide](/guides/research). It covers finding labs, reaching out to faculty, and understanding the research process in more detail.

## ASU-backed project courses

Some of the most accessible team-based project experiences are courses you can enroll in directly. These options offer academic credit, a built-in team structure, and a concrete deliverable by the end of the semester.

- **[EPICS — FSE 104 / FSE 404](https://epics.engineering.asu.edu/)** (Engineering Projects in Community Service): Work on a multidisciplinary team supporting a real community partner, such as a nonprofit, school, or government organization. FSE 104 is a 1-credit introductory course open to undergraduates, while FSE 404 is the advanced repeatable version. Both also count as honors credit for Barrett students.
- **FSE 301 — Entrepreneur & Value Creation**: A useful option for students who want to turn technical ideas into projects with practical or market value.
- **Capstone courses** (CSE 485/486 for CS, along with other senior capstones by major): These courses place you on a team building a project for an industry or faculty sponsor. Since experiences can vary depending on the sponsor, it can be helpful to ask older students about their experiences.
- **Honors theses and directed study (CSE 492/493, FSE 492/493)**: If you already have a faculty mentor, these courses can allow you to earn credit for project work you are doing with them.

## Finding local startups

Large tech internships can be highly competitive. Local startups can sometimes offer a more accessible path to meaningful experience, especially for undergraduates. Because teams are smaller, the work is often more hands-on and can give you a better sense of how products and companies operate day to day.

- **Where to look:** Handshake and LinkedIn both allow you to filter by company size and location. Searching in the Phoenix, Tempe, and Scottsdale area for smaller companies can be a good starting point.
- **Consider reaching out directly.** Smaller startups often do not have formal recruiting pipelines, so a short, thoughtful email to a founder or lead engineer can sometimes be effective. It helps to show that you understand what the company does and can point to a specific way you might contribute.
- **Attend local meetups and demo nights.** Events such as Phoenix Startup Week, PHX Startup Grind, and pitch events hosted by the programs below can be good places to meet founders and teams.
- **Be cautious with unpaid offers.** Some opportunities, especially equity-only roles from unfamiliar founders, may not provide meaningful mentorship or structure. Approach these carefully and evaluate whether the experience is likely to be worthwhile.

## ASU-specific startup programs

ASU has a well-developed entrepreneurship ecosystem, and many of its programs are open to students across majors. These can be especially useful if you want to build your own project, explore commercialization, or meet potential collaborators.

<details>
<summary>E+I @ Fulton Schools</summary>

The Entrepreneurship + Innovation program within the Fulton Schools of Engineering. This is a strong entry point for engineering students interested in startups, venture building, or product development. The program offers classes, pitch competitions, and access to **Generator Labs**, a makerspace and coworking space located in ECG 101 on the Tempe campus.

- Website: [entrepreneurship.engineering.asu.edu](https://entrepreneurship.engineering.asu.edu/)

</details>

<details>
<summary>Venture Devils</summary>

ASU’s university-wide student startup support program, run through the Edson E+I Institute. Students can participate at any stage, whether they have a polished idea, an early concept, or are still exploring.

What the program offers:

- **Funding:** Pitch competitions such as Pitch Playoffs and Demo Day, with awards that are often substantial for student ventures
- **Mentorship:** Access to experienced entrepreneur mentors across a range of industries
- **Workspaces and perks:** Innovation spaces and a library of free or discounted business resources
- **Eligibility:** At least one founder must be a current ASU or Maricopa Community Colleges student; students from any major or academic level may participate

If you have a project that could realistically develop into a product or venture, this is often one of the most valuable programs to explore.

- Website: [venturedevils.asu.edu](https://venturedevils.asu.edu/)

</details>

<details>
<summary>Skysong Innovations</summary>

Skysong Innovations is ASU’s technology transfer and commercialization arm. It supports the process of moving research toward patents, licensing, and startup formation. Many undergraduates encounter it indirectly through faculty research, Venture Devils, or related programs, but it is useful to know about if your work has commercialization potential.

- Website: [skysonginnovations.com](https://skysonginnovations.com/)

</details>

<details>
<summary>ASU Tech Devils & Tech Venture Challenge</summary>

These are more advanced opportunities for Venture Devils participants who have already demonstrated traction. Tech Devils includes an innovation retreat, while the Hool Coury Law Tech Venture Challenge offers seed grant funding. Participation generally begins through Venture Devils.

</details>

<details>
<summary>ASU DevSpace & Shojaee Software Startup Challenge</summary>

These opportunities are especially relevant for software-focused founders. Supported by DevLabs at ASU, they are designed for students who want guidance on building a software company beyond the hackathon stage.

</details>

## Joining an existing student project

Starting your own project is not the only path. In many cases, joining an existing project can be one of the fastest ways to gain meaningful experience, especially if the team already has momentum and structure.

A few ways to find those opportunities:

- **Student organization projects:** Groups such as SoDA, CodeDevils, Claude Builder Club, AIS, and GDSC often run semester-long team projects. Joining their Discords and watching for project kickoff announcements at the start of the semester can help.
- **Hackathon teams that continue after the event:** SunHacks and other local hackathons often produce teams that keep building after the event ends. If you see a project you like, reaching out to the team can be worthwhile.
- **Open source on campus:** ASULUG and various student-run GitHub organizations, including the group maintaining this wiki, often welcome contributors. Open source can be a good way to get experience with code review, collaboration, and public-facing project work.

## Tips for making project work count

- **Choose projects you can discuss in depth.** In interviews, you will often be asked to walk through a project. Strong projects are usually the ones you can explain clearly, including your role, tradeoffs, and outcomes.
- **Finish what you start.** A smaller completed project is usually more valuable than a much larger unfinished one.
- **Document your work as you go.** Good READMEs, notes, screenshots, and design decisions become useful later when updating your resume, portfolio, or applications.
- **Work with strong collaborators.** One of the fastest ways to improve is by joining teams where you can learn from others’ technical and organizational habits.
